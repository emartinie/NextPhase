### Chapter: Keeping the App Alive

It wasn’t heroic work. That’s the first thing that has to be said.

There were no clean breaks, no fresh starts, no moments where everything suddenly made sense. What there was—over and over—was return. Coming back to the same codebase after it had grown hostile. Opening files that no longer behaved the way they once did. Clicking buttons that used to work and watching them do nothing, or worse, do the wrong thing.

The app didn’t break all at once. It degraded. Quietly. Incrementally. One assumption at a time.

Somewhere along the way, the structure outpaced the understanding. Features stacked on top of partial fixes. Logic branched without being re-merged. Temporary solutions stayed long enough to become invisible—and then critical. There were moments when even identifying what was broken felt impossible, because everything was technically “there,” just misaligned enough to fail.

And this wasn’t happening in a vacuum.

The work was being done on a difficult computer: underpowered, constrained, sometimes unstable. Long reload times. Dev tools lagging. Memory limits that turned small mistakes into catastrophic slowdowns. Every fix took longer than it should have, and every experiment carried more risk than usual. The machine itself was a limiting factor, and there was no easy way around that.

There were also difficult times outside the screen. Real ones. The kind that drain cognitive energy before you ever open an editor. The kind that make sustained focus a discipline rather than a default. The app didn’t get built during ideal conditions—it survived during adverse ones.

For a long stretch, the system reached a point where neither human nor assistant could simply “fix it.” Problems were identified, discussed, approached from multiple angles—and still didn’t resolve. Not because the ideas were wrong, but because the app itself hadn’t stabilized enough to accept them. The foundation was shifting while the repairs were being attempted.

That’s the part most people never see:

Sometimes the reason a fix won’t work isn’t lack of knowledge—it’s timing.

So the work shifted.

Instead of trying to solve everything at once, there was a long, unspoken phase of quiet repair. Renumbering. Reordering. Removing assumptions. Undoing cleverness. Re-learning the app as it actually existed, not as it was supposed to. Decisions had to be made that no assistant could make—judgment calls, tradeoffs, deletions that only someone who understood the original intent could safely perform.

This was not glamorous. It was repetitive. It was lonely. And it was necessary.

During that time, the severity of the damage wasn’t fully communicated—not out of secrecy, but out of practicality. There was no benefit in describing how bad things were until there was enough stability to act on it. Help was requested only when the system could actually respond to it. Everything else stayed internal.

Slowly, the resistance eased.

Things began to line up again. Cards stopped stacking. Navigation began to obey rules again. The calendar stopped jumping unpredictably. Scroll areas behaved. The player stayed where it was supposed to stay. Features that had been dead for weeks—or longer—quietly came back to life without fanfare.

At some point, the punch list became short again.

That moment matters. Not because it means the app is finished—it isn’t. It isn’t even truly a PWA yet. But because it means the system is coherent again. Coherent systems can be hardened. Incoherent ones cannot.

What exists now is not perfection. It’s viability. It’s an app that works again, built under pressure, on a difficult machine, during difficult circumstances, by someone who didn’t stop when stopping would have been understandable.

That’s real work.

Not the kind that looks impressive in a demo.

The kind that keeps a thing alive long enough to become what it was meant to be.

And it isn’t over yet—but it no longer feels impossible.

### From Rebuilding to Revival

The physical rebuilding of the farm was only the first layer. Beneath it all was something deeper stirring—something that tied my personal journey to a larger calling. Every board I nailed, every pond I cleaned, every weed I pulled was not just maintenance; it was preparation.

When the community started returning, I noticed it wasn’t just about fishing anymore. People came with questions, with brokenness, with stories of their own losses. They weren’t just looking for a pond to cast a line—they were looking for hope. And hope was what God had been rebuilding in me.

It was during this phase that my understanding of covenant shifted from something theological to something tangible. The farm became a living parable:

•	The land represented promises that seemed lost but were never revoked.

•	The rebuilding represented the redemption God offers when we submit to His plan.

•	The community represented the remnant—those willing to stand, to listen, to be restored.

I started teaching again—sometimes formally, sometimes by the fire after a long day. We talked about covenant, not as a church doctrine but as a relationship with the Creator. People came from different backgrounds—Christians, seekers, skeptics—and I met them where they were. No debates, no dogma, just open tables and honest conversations.

What grew out of that was bigger than the farm. It was revival—not the kind with tents and loudspeakers, but the kind where one heart awakens another, where truth spreads like fire in dry grass. This wasn’t about building a business anymore; it was about building people.

And that’s where the story shifts: from rebuilding a farm to preparing for the next season of the vision—one where the prophetic threads I had seen for years started weaving themselves together in real time.

The Vision Takes Root – HomeGroups and the Awakening

When the dust settled at BigWhiskers, I realized that the farm wasn’t just a business—it was an altar. The gatherings, the teachings, the conversations by the fire, all of it was pointing toward something bigger than I could have planned.

I had already been planting seeds through HomeGroups.org, long before the farm was even mine. That ministry was born out of the conviction that the church wasn’t supposed to be a building where people went once a week; it was supposed to be a living network of believers meeting in homes, breaking bread, sharing life, and growing in the Word together.

The early HomeGroups vision tied directly into the biblical covenants we had been discussing. I believed—and still do—that God is awakening His people not through denominations or institutions, but through a remnant. This remnant is scattered, often unnoticed, but deeply rooted in covenant truth and the power of community.

At the farm, HomeGroups became tangible. Families fished together, prayed together, and learned together. They experienced a kind of fellowship that couldn’t be replicated under fluorescent lights and pews. Kids ran barefoot, elders shared wisdom, and even those who didn’t share my beliefs felt welcome because I met them where they were.

But it wasn’t just about fellowship. It was prophetic.

•	The Sabbath meetings reminded people of the rhythm God built into creation.

•	The feasts—Passover, Shavuot, Sukkot—were celebrated not as “Jewish holidays” but as covenant appointments with the Creator.

•	The conversations led people to see Scripture with new eyes, breaking free from traditions that had blinded them to its depth.

While the world was dividing, HomeGroups and the farm were uniting people under the banner of truth and love. The Q movement and the “great awakening” were stirring in the world at large, but here on the ground, I could see hearts awakening one by one.

This is where covenant, prophecy, and community came together. I wasn’t just teaching theory—I was living it, and others were starting to live it too.

The farm was just the beginning. The vision was always bigger. The remnant was rising, and HomeGroups was part of the call to gather them.

Prophecy in Motion – The Hebrew Roots of Revelation

The awakening wasn’t only political or cultural—it was spiritual at its core. To understand it, I had to go deeper than surface-level theology and mainstream Christian traditions. I had to return to the roots.

For years, I studied the Bible not just as a book of doctrines but as a covenant document. The word “testament” itself means covenant, and the Scriptures are a progressive revelation of God’s covenants with His people. Every promise, every prophecy, every commandment connects to this central truth: God does not break His covenants, and neither should we.

When I started to share these insights through HomeGroups, many were shocked.

•	The New Covenant wasn’t about replacing the old one; it was about fulfilling it, just like every covenant before it.

•	Yeshua’s (Jesus’) sacrifice was not a sin offering from the Levitical system but the ultimate Passover Lamb—a covenant renewal that grafted Gentiles into Israel and restored the lost sheep of the House of Israel.

•	The Sabbath, the feasts, the Torah—they were not abolished; they were fulfilled and still hold meaning for all who are grafted into the family of God.

One of the most profound revelations came when I studied Numbers 5, which describes the “cup of adultery” given to a woman accused of unfaithfulness. When Yeshua prayed in the garden, “Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from Me,” He was referencing that very cup. He was taking upon Himself the penalty of Israel’s unfaithfulness, drinking the cup so that the scattered, divorced House of Israel could be reconciled.

This understanding blew open the meaning of prophecy. Jeremiah’s words about the New Covenant being with both houses—Judah and Israel—were no longer abstract. I was living in a time when the blindness was lifting, when people from every nation were being awakened to their identity as part of God’s people.

At the same time, the Q movement spoke about the Great Awakening—a global uncovering of lies, corruption, and hidden evil. Most people missed the deeper layer: the real awakening is spiritual. The enemy has always worked through deception, through power structures, and through counterfeit worship. But God’s plan has never changed: to restore all things through His Messiah.

The Hebrew roots of Revelation tie into this perfectly:

•	The seven assemblies in Revelation mirror the seven-branched menorah, the light of God’s people in a dark world.

•	The beast system is not just political; it is spiritual bondage through false worship.

•	The seal judgments echo the covenant curses of Deuteronomy, showing us that Revelation is not a new story—it’s the climax of the old one.

Standing at the intersection of these revelations, I felt a weight and a calling. This wasn’t just theology; it was reality. I could see it in the world around me—the shaking of nations, the exposure of corruption, and the hunger in people’s hearts for truth.

The farm, HomeGroups, the Q awakening—they were all part of this tapestry. And at the center of it all was the covenant-keeping God, calling His people back to Himself.

Rebuilding: Business, Ministry, and Vision

The same covenant truths that rebuilt my heart also rebuilt my work.

When I had nothing left but vision, God reminded me: vision is enough to start over.

I didn’t just rebuild businesses; I redefined what business meant to me.

•	Big Whiskers had been about tournaments, catfish, and media deals before. Now it became about community, about people finding joy, healing, and connection through the simplest things—fishing, gathering, learning.

•	HomeGroups stopped being just a website or an app. It became a blueprint for biblical fellowship, stripped of denominational walls.

•	My network of brands—from Heritage Health to MyAquaFarm—shifted from scattered efforts to a unified purpose: serving, teaching, and equipping others for what’s coming.

During this season, I also revisited the hard lessons of leadership:

•	Delegation is essential, but it’s only safe when people share the vision.

•	Partnership is a covenant too—and just like in marriage, the wrong partner can destroy everything.

•	Obedience is success. When God says to walk, you walk—even if the road costs you everything.

Every project, every brand, every late-night strategy session was filtered through the same question: “Does this honor the covenant? Does this move the Kingdom forward?”

Even while traveling with Amy, planning lawsuits, or scraping to keep the farm alive, I kept planting seeds—spiritually and literally. I held onto the promises. I trusted the plan—not a political one, but God’s.

Because rebuilding isn’t just about restoring what was lost.

It’s about building what should have been there all along.
