### Chapter:The Inheritance I Never Had

I never received an inheritance.

No land folded into my hands with a handshake and a deed.

No envelope with a check and a letter that said, We always believed in you.

No family cabin tucked into the woods with my name penciled quietly into a will.

No stocks. No savings bonds. No quiet account accruing interest in my absence.

Not even advice.

No one sat me down and said, Here’s how you build something that lasts.

No one handed me a map with the roads already marked in red.

No one explained how to protect what I earned, how to structure it, how to guard it from storms I couldn’t yet see.

I inherited no money.

No property.

No tools.

No blueprint.

If anything, I inherited confusion — and the quiet understanding that I would have to learn everything the hard way.

And maybe that is why I was so determined to change the story.

For most of my adult life, I carried a private pride: I would be the interruption. The break in the pattern. The first man in my line to build something that could not be dismissed as temporary. I achieved what I set out to achieve. I climbed. I earned. I became the CIO at Gibson Guitar. I made the six-figure salary. I proved — at least to myself — that starting with nothing did not mean finishing with nothing.

Every dollar I earned felt heavier because it had not been inherited. It had been fought for. Every asset had sweat in it. Every promotion had late nights in it. Every opportunity had risk in it.

I built brick by brick.

And at home, I built something more fragile and more important.

My wife worked — and she worked hard — but the economics of our life were complicated. Homeschool networks, church involvement, side ventures, small business attempts, the culture of it all. Money flowed out in ways that didn’t always return. And yet, I cannot deny that she was a devoted homeschool mother. I did not want her to carry the burden of earning; I wanted her to carry the freedom of nurturing. I wanted the children to enjoy church, not feel dragged there because of obligation. I wanted our home to be a sanctuary, not a production line.

I wanted stability.

But because I had never received an inheritance, I became consumed with creating one.

Not for me.

For my children.

For both of them — but especially for my son.

I imagined him one day standing where I once stood, only higher. I imagined him looking at what I had built and knowing he would never have to start from zero the way I did. I had watched how help seemed to appear for others in my life — apartments provided, safety nets offered, quiet interventions made. I did not receive those. And whether that was fairness or circumstance no longer matters. What mattered was the fear that my son might face the same silence.

I didn’t just want to leave him money.

I wanted to leave him margin.

I wanted to leave him safety.

I wanted to leave him proof that his father had altered the Martinie trajectory — that we had, in some small way, changed our stars.

When I bought property, I didn’t see it as mine. I saw it as his future.

The house.

The garden.

The greenhouse.

The land.

The barn.

The ponds stocked with fish.

The tools hanging in their places.

The vehicles, motorcycles, jet skis, the ATVs.

These were not indulgences. They were building blocks. They were demonstrations. They were a statement: We are no longer starting from nothing.

When I saved money, it was not for comfort. It was for continuity. When I told my son, standing in a store, that we could buy what we needed, it was not pride. It was reassurance. It was love translated into provision.

When I worked late, it was not ambition.

It was architecture.

Every possession was a brick.

Every sacrifice was mortar.

Every plan was scaffolding around a house he would someday inherit and build upon.

I imagined the conversation.

This is mys now.

The house. The farm. The enterprises. I built it for you. I’ll stay and help if you want, but you’re in charge.

It wasn’t ego.

It was redemption.

Because I knew what it felt like to start empty.

I knew the humiliation of having no safety net. I knew the anxiety of one unexpected bill undoing months of discipline. I knew what it felt like to enter adulthood with no cushion beneath me.

I wanted my son to stand on my shoulders, not beside me in the dirt.

And then I lost it.

Not slowly.

Not ceremonially.

Not with enough warning to brace my heart.

I lost the assets.

The progress.

The stability.

The structure that had taken years to assemble.

Loss is one thing when it belongs to you. It is something else when it belongs to my children.

What broke my heart was not the disappearance of property.

It was the silence afterward.

The realization that the inheritance I had been building was gone.

I had been homeless before in my life. I knew how to survive lean seasons. I knew how to rebuild. I knew how to adapt. But this time it was different. This time it felt like I had failed a future version of my son. It felt like I had become the version of myself I once feared.

There is a particular kind of pain in losing something that was never meant to be mys in the first place. When you lose what you built for myself, it stings. When you lose what you built for my children, it fractures something deeper.

I replayed decisions.

I replayed risks.

I replayed moments where I might have been more cautious, more strategic, more patient.

Every replay ended with the same image: the house I built in my mind standing empty.

And yet — even in the wreckage — something refused to die.

Because while I never inherited land or money or property or advice… I also never inherited surrender.

No one handed me resilience.

I had to discover that.

And slowly, painfully, I began to remember something essential:

Inheritance is not only what you leave.

It is what you model.

Even when I had no house, I called my children. Every day. I tried to see them whenever I could. Sometimes that meant showing up with drinks and snacks after work, even if I had little else to offer. Presence became my currency. Effort became my estate.

My children know that I never stopped building. Even when the day was over and exhaustion pressed in, I was still planning, still repairing, still imagining the next foundation.

If my son inherits nothing else from me — no deed, no account, no tangible asset — he will inherit this:

I build.

And when I lose, I build again.

He will inherit the knowledge that his father once had everything and lost it — and did not disappear. He will inherit the understanding that monetary wealth can collapse, but character must not. He will inherit the memory of watching a man try to rise again, even when the room is empty and no one applauds the effort.

It still aches.

It aches when I imagine the easier life I wanted to secure for him.

It aches when I fear time slipping past before I can rebuild something visible again.

It aches because I wanted to hand him something solid and say, You start higher than I did.

But maybe the deeper inheritance isn’t height.

Maybe it’s strength.

Strength forged honestly — through hardship, not comfort.

Maybe it’s vision.

Maybe it’s the refusal to let loss define the legacy.

I never received an inheritance.

But I am still becoming one.

The estate may be gone.

The structures may have fallen.

The visible proof may have vanished.

But the legacy?

The legacy is still under construction.
