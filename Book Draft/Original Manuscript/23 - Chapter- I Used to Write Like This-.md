### Chapter: I Used to Write Like This?

Every once in a while I stumble across something I wrote twenty years ago.

Sometimes I smile.

Sometimes I cringe.

This was one of those moments.

I found an old description of HomeGroups that confidently promised to break down every denominational wall, expose the errors of nearly every church, audit congregations, and teach people more in forty days than they might have learned in forty years.

When I read it today, I laughed.

Not because it was written with bad intentions.

It wasn't.

It was written by someone who desperately wanted people to know the truth.

The problem wasn't my desire for truth.

It was my understanding of people.

I've noticed something over the years.

As we grow in knowledge, we often become less patient with those who haven't yet reached the same conclusions.

It's an easy trap to fall into.

We confuse being convinced with being called to convince everyone else.

Looking back, I can hear that younger version of myself trying to win arguments before earning trust.

Trying to correct before listening.

Trying to persuade before understanding.

Today I still care deeply about truth.

Maybe more than I ever have.

In fact, I hold many convictions more strongly today than I did when I wrote those words.

But something else has changed.

I've learned that being right and being loving are not competing virtues.

God never asked me to choose between them.

He asked me to grow in both.

One of the greatest transformations in my life hasn't been what I believe.

It's how I hold those beliefs.

I used to think maturity meant becoming more certain.

Now I think maturity means becoming more humble while remaining convinced.

There's a difference.

Humility doesn't require uncertainty.

It requires remembering that I have been wrong before.

Many times.

I've spent years studying Scripture, history, language, archaeology, and theology.

I've changed my mind on more subjects than I can count.

Some of those changes happened slowly.

Others happened over dinner.

More than once I've sat down absolutely convinced I was right and intentionally tried to prove myself wrong.

Sometimes I succeeded.

Those moments didn't weaken my faith.

They strengthened it.

Truth has nothing to fear from honest investigation.

I've also learned that every person I meet is standing somewhere along the same road I've been walking.

Some are a few steps behind me.

Others are miles ahead.

Most are simply trying to follow God as faithfully as they know how.

If someone is sincerely seeking Him, I no longer see them as an opponent to defeat.

I see them as a fellow traveler.

The older I get, the less interested I become in winning theological debates.

Not because doctrine doesn't matter.

It does.

Deeply.

But I've discovered that people rarely change because they lose an argument.

They change because someone loved them enough to keep asking questions with them.

If I could sit down with the man who wrote that original HomeGroups description, I wouldn't criticize him.

I'd thank him.

His passion built the foundation that I still stand on today.

Then I'd put a hand on his shoulder and tell him something he couldn't yet see.

"Keep pursuing truth with everything you've got.

Just remember...

The people you're trying to convince are not my enemies.

They're my neighbors."

Maybe that's what spiritual maturity looks like.

Not abandoning conviction.

Learning to carry conviction with grace.

Because at the end of the day, my goal isn't to build people who think exactly like me.

My goal is to help people love God enough to keep seeking Him.

If they do that honestly, I trust the same God who has patiently corrected me for decades will continue His work in them as well.
