### Chapter: I Was Wrong

There was a time in my life when I secretly believed that maturity looked like certainty.

The more certain I became, the more mature I assumed I was.

If I could answer every question, quote every verse, defend every doctrine, and explain every difficult passage, then surely I was growing.

I wasn't.

I was accumulating information.

Growth came later.

I have spent a lifetime chasing truth.

Not because I enjoy arguments.

Not because I wanted to prove people wrong.

I genuinely wanted to know God.

Somewhere along the journey, however, I confused confidence with completeness.

I thought that once I reached the right conclusion, I had arrived.

What I eventually discovered is that God wasn't nearly as interested in my arrival as He was in my willingness to keep walking.

I've changed my mind more times than I can count.

Some changes were small.

Others completely rearranged the way I understood Scripture.

Some took years.

Others happened in a single evening because someone asked one question I couldn't answer.

I wish I could tell you I welcomed those moments.

Usually I didn't.

Usually they hurt.

Because changing my mind isn't just changing an opinion.

Sometimes it's admitting you've built part of my identity around an idea that simply wasn't true.

One of the greatest fears we carry is the fear of being wrong.

Especially in matters of faith.

If we've taught something...

Defended something...

Built friendships around something...

Or maybe even built a ministry around something...

Changing our minds can feel like losing part of ourselves.

I've been there.

More than once.

Here's what surprised me.

Every time I surrendered an idea that couldn't withstand honest investigation, I didn't lose God.

I found Him.

Again.

Truth has never been threatened by my questions.

Only my pride has.

For years I thought my responsibility was to defend everything I believed.

Today I believe my responsibility is much simpler.

Follow the evidence wherever it leads, and trust that God isn't waiting for me at the end of my favorite doctrine.

He's waiting at the end of the truth.

Those aren't always the same place.

I have friends who disagree with me.

Good friends.

Some think I'm too conservative.

Others think I'm too progressive.

Some believe I've abandoned tradition.

Others think I haven't abandoned enough of it.

Years ago, that would've bothered me.

Today it doesn't.

Not because truth doesn't matter.

It matters immensely.

But because I've learned that every sincere believer is somewhere on a journey.

None of us started in the same place.

None of us learned the same lessons.

None of us received the same teaching.

Why should I expect everyone to arrive at the same conclusions on the same day?

One of the biggest changes in my life hasn't been what I believe.

It's how I carry those beliefs.

When I was younger, I often felt an urgency to correct people.

Now I feel an urgency to understand them.

That doesn't mean I no longer challenge ideas.

I do.

Sometimes vigorously.

But I've learned there's a profound difference between challenging an idea and attacking the person holding it.

One invites conversation.

The other ends it.

I used to think every disagreement needed a winner.

Now I think every disagreement deserves a listener.

I've had conversations with atheists who showed more intellectual honesty than some believers.

I've met lifelong Christians who loved Jesus deeply while holding conclusions very different from my own.

I've learned from Jewish scholars who don't share my view of Messiah.

I've learned from pastors whose traditions differ from mine.

I've learned from historians, linguists, archaeologists, scientists, skeptics, and friends who simply asked good questions.

God has never limited Himself to teaching me through people who agree with me.

Why should I?

There's a phrase I come back to often.

Hold my convictions with open hands.

Not loose hands.

Not careless hands.

Open hands.

There's a difference.

Loose hands don't care what they hold.

Open hands are willing to let God remove something if it doesn't belong there.

I want convictions strong enough to stand against criticism.

But I also want humility strong enough to survive correction.

Over the years I've noticed something curious.

The people most confident that they have nothing left to learn usually stop learning.

The people who continue asking questions often discover truths they never expected to find.

I don't want to become the first person.

Not at seventy.

Not at eighty.

Not on my last day.

If God has something new to teach me tomorrow, I hope I still have the courage to say three simple words.

I was wrong.

That sentence used to feel like defeat.

Today it feels like freedom.

Because every time I've admitted I was wrong, I've made room to become a little more right.

Not because I suddenly became smarter.

Because I became more teachable.

And I have a suspicion that teachability is one of the most overlooked marks of spiritual maturity.

HomeGroups was never built because I believed I had all the answers.

It was built because I kept discovering better questions.

Every investigation, every timeline, every language study, every historical rabbit trail, every comparison between traditions began the same way.

"I wonder if that's really true."

Sometimes the answer confirmed what I already believed.

Sometimes it dismantled years of assumptions.

I learned to celebrate both outcomes.

If an investigation strengthens the truth, wonderful.

If it exposes one of my errors, that's wonderful too.

Either way, I end the day knowing God a little better than I did yesterday.

If you read this book hoping I'll never be wrong, I'm afraid you'll be disappointed.

I will almost certainly write something that future Eddie wishes he had phrased differently.

I may even discover that some conclusion I hold today needs revision tomorrow.

That doesn't frighten me anymore.

It encourages me.

Because it means I'm still learning.

What I can promise you is something different.

I'll do my best to tell you why I believe what I believe.

I'll show you where I found the evidence.

I'll invite you to examine it myself.

And if you discover I've missed something important...

I hope you'll tell me.

Not because I enjoy being corrected.

No one does.

But because I love the truth more than I love being right.

If I've learned anything over the years, it's this:

God is remarkably patient with people who are sincerely seeking Him.

I want to become one of those people.

And I hope, by the time you finish this book, you'll want to become one too.
