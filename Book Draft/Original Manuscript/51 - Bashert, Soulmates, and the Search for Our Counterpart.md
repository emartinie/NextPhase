### Bashert, Soulmates, and the Search for Our Counterpart

One of the most beautiful concepts I've encountered in Jewish tradition is the idea of bashert (sometimes discussed alongside zivug), the belief that every person has a divinely appointed counterpart.

According to Jewish tradition, before a person is born, their soul is announced in heaven, and their future spouse—their bashert—is identified. The idea rests on the belief that every individual has a unique purpose and destiny, and that discovering one's counterpart is part of fulfilling that purpose.

A beautiful tradition also teaches that when a soul is created, it is divided into two halves. Each half is sent into the world in a different body, and throughout life we search for the other half. Whether taken literally or symbolically, it is a profound picture of longing, purpose, and connection.

The concept extends beyond marriage. Many believe that bashert also applies to life's deepest friendships, partnerships, mentors, and companions. It reminds us that meaningful relationships may not be accidental but part of a much larger story unfolding over time.

I don't expect everyone to agree with this perspective, but I find it compelling because it reflects another ancient idea: there is no one without their counterpart. Every person has a unique role to play, and every meaningful relationship has the potential to shape that calling.

Over the years I've also explored ideas such as soulmates and, more recently, the modern concept of twin flames. While I appreciate the questions these ideas raise, I have developed my own working theory.

I suspect the soul is more complex than a single, indivisible thing. My current thought is that it may consist of multiple dimensions or facets. If that is true, perhaps two people do not need to perfectly align in every dimension to become soulmates. A profound connection in even one part of the soul could create a relationship that changes both lives forever.

If there are, for example, seven major dimensions of the soul, then perhaps each person has more than one potential soulmate. One extraordinary relationship may touch every part of our being, while another may deeply connect with only one or two. Both can be genuine. Both can be life-changing.

I don't present this as doctrine. It is simply a working hypothesis born from observation, study, and experience.

For most of my life, I have searched for that kind of connection. I have never been interested in casual relationships or meaningless intimacy. Even as a teenager, I found myself searching for something much deeper.

Life has given me both heartbreak and extraordinary love. After my marriage ended, someone who had quietly loved me for decades drove hundreds of miles to begin a life with me. That relationship didn't last forever, but it taught me something invaluable: real love exists. Once you've experienced it, you know the difference.

That experience changed my expectations forever.

Today, I continue to believe that meaningful relationships are among God's greatest gifts. Whether we call them bashert, soulmates, kindred spirits, or simply faithful companions, they remind us that we were created for relationship—not merely to avoid loneliness, but to become better, wiser, and more complete through one another.

Perhaps the search for our counterpart is not merely about finding another person.

Perhaps it is also part of discovering who we were created to become.
