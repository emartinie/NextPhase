### Interlude: The Covenant Thread

Understanding the Bible is all about covenant.

The word testament itself simply means covenant—and there are far more than two covenants in Scripture. The Adamic, Noahide, Abrahamic, Davidic, Mosaic, and New Covenants each build on one another, like a series of vows made by God to humanity. They are not either/or, as so many modern Christians believe, but one before the other—each fulfilled in sequence, each revealing more of the heart of the Father.

The New Testament cannot be understood without the Old. The apostles who wrote its words carried scrolls of the Torah and the Prophets; none of them possessed what we now call a “New Testament.” Even the voice of Yeshua (Jesus) echoed the same message of covenant faithfulness that 48 prophets had proclaimed before Him.

And how easily we forget: the very name Jesus is a late invention, the sound of “J” not existing until the 1500s. His Hebrew name, Yeshua, ties Him directly to salvation and the covenant promises of Israel.

Covenants always begin with faith. They are not works-based ladders to heaven, but relationships marked by loyalty and love. The Old Covenant’s sacrificial system was never a way to earn salvation; it was for those already in covenant, already saved by the blood of the Passover lamb. Yeshua fulfilled this as the ultimate Passover sacrifice—not a sin offering from the law, but the Lamb of God whose blood delivers us from death itself.

The Sabbath—created at the dawn of time—was given as a sign, like a wedding ring, marking those who are set apart. Yeshua Himself declared that the Sabbath was made for mankind and that He is Lord of it. Yet nowhere in Scripture is there a verse that changes the Sabbath to another day. The covenant sign remains.

The truth is simple: our obedience is not an attempt to earn salvation. It is the evidence that the kingdom of heaven is alive and working in us.

This is the heartbeat of covenant—like marriage, like a sacred vow, binding us to God and to one another. It’s not law versus grace, old versus new. It is a love story, unfolding chapter by chapter, generation by generation, until the fullness of the nations comes in.

Interlude: The Cup and the Two Houses

Covenant is the thread that ties all of Scripture together. Without understanding covenants, you cannot understand the Bible. And without understanding the prophetic picture of the two houses—Israel and Judah—you miss the heart of God’s redemptive plan.

The prophet Jeremiah is explicit: it was Israel—the northern kingdom—that was given a certificate of divorce because of her spiritual adultery. Judah, the southern kingdom, remained in covenant, though she was chastised for her own rebellion. Mainstream Christianity often collapses this distinction, but Jeremiah 31 and 16 show us that the promised new covenant is made with both houses. It’s not a brand-new religion—it’s a restoration, a grafting back in, a reunion.

This is where the story of salvation explodes with meaning. The northern kingdom’s unfaithfulness became the doorway for the nations—the gentiles—to be brought in. Paul understood this mystery when he wrote of gentiles being grafted into the olive tree (Romans 11). Salvation extended outward because of Israel’s fall, but the end of the story is their reconciliation.

When Yeshua (Jesus) prayed in Gethsemane, “Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me,” most sermons barely touch what that cup really was. The answer lies in Numbers 5—the cup of bitterness, the cup of adultery given to an unfaithful wife to test her. Yeshua was drinking the cup of Israel’s adultery, taking on the judgment she deserved, so she could be restored. The cross was not only a payment for sin but the fulfillment of the ancient betrothal vow.

This is why understanding the two houses is central to understanding the gospel. It’s why the Sabbath remains a sign of covenant. It’s why the promises of God stand firm—because they are to both houses, and through them, to the entire world.
