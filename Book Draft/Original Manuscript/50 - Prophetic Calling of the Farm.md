### Prophetic Calling of the Farm

Looking back, it’s clear that my farm wasn’t just a business—it was prophetic ground. Like Ezekiel standing over the valley of dry bones, I was standing on a piece of land that had seen life, death, and decay. The tournaments, the catfish ponds, the old business models—all of it felt like dry bones waiting for the breath of God.

Ezekiel 37 says:

“Prophesy to these bones and say to them, ‘Dry bones, hear the word of the Lord! I will make breath enter you, and you will come to life.’”

That’s what I felt happening there. Even as I wrestled with my convictions—the Sabbath, the food laws, the tension between what was and what should be—I knew God was breathing something new. The very things that felt contradictory became the canvas for a bigger picture: redemption.

People didn’t just come to fish; they came to belong. They came to heal. They came to make memories. The farm became a safe place where families could laugh, where kids could experience wonder, where the elderly could feel valued, and where the broken could breathe.

Every Saturday tournament, even with its conflicts, felt like prophecy in motion: life was being spoken into dry bones. What started as an ordinary catfish farm became something sacred—a place where heaven touched earth in ways no business plan could have predicted.

The Sabbath Dilemma

When I finally owned the farm, I inherited more than land—I inherited a system already built on practices that clashed with my convictions. The tournaments were always on Saturdays, and that was their biggest draw. But for me, Friday sundown to Saturday sundown was the Sabbath, a time set apart for rest and worship.

On top of that, the farm’s main revenue stream came from catfish and pork. Catfish, according to my understanding of the Bible, is not food. Neither is pork. Yet here I was, the new owner of a business that not only profited from these things but was built on them.

I was torn. Everything about the farm’s success was tangled in things I didn’t fully agree with. Yet, deep down, I felt led—like God had placed me there because of this tension, not in spite of it. This wasn’t about compromise; it was about stewardship. I wasn’t there to shut everything down overnight. I was there to transform, slowly, faithfully, and in His timing.

The farm became a living lesson: sometimes you inherit a world that doesn’t line up perfectly with your beliefs, and you have to navigate it one step at a time. Instead of forcing immediate change, I focused on creating an environment that honored God where I could—through hospitality, teaching, and building community.

In time, the farm itself started to shift. The fishing tournaments weren’t just competitions; they became gatherings where families, kids, and even the elderly found joy and connection. The place started to reflect the Kingdom, even while still existing in a world that isn’t fully redeemed.
