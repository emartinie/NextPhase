### The Cup and the Wedding Feast

At the Last Supper, Yeshua lifted the cup and said, “This cup is the new covenant in My blood, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins” (Luke 22:20). Many have assumed He was inventing a “new Christian ritual,” but that night was a Passover meal—a covenant meal that Israel had celebrated for centuries.

The cup He held was not random; it was the third cup of the Passover seder, known as the Cup of Redemption. This cup symbolized God’s promise in Exodus 6:6–7 to redeem His people. When Yeshua said He would not drink again of this fruit of the vine until He drank it with His disciples in His Father’s Kingdom, He was pointing forward to the final cup—the Cup of Praise—which would be fulfilled at the Wedding Supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19:6–9).

Every covenant in Scripture is marked by a meal, a shared table where parties confirm their relationship. From Abraham eating with the three visitors, to Israel’s elders eating in God’s presence at Sinai, to this moment in the upper room—the pattern is consistent: covenant is sealed with fellowship.

When Yeshua took the bread and wine, He was not discarding the Torah or starting a “Christian ordinance.” He was fulfilling the prophetic meaning of the Passover: the Lamb’s blood delivers, redeems, and brings you into covenant with God. This meal was—and still is—a Hebrew covenant meal.

The Bridegroom and the Bride

In Jewish wedding tradition, the groom would share a cup of wine with his bride at betrothal, promising to return for her and complete the marriage. He would say something very similar to Yeshua’s words: “I will not drink of this cup again until I drink it with you in my father’s house.”

This is exactly what Yeshua was saying:

“I am the Bridegroom. I am leaving to prepare a place for you. I will come again. And when I do, we will drink this cup together in My Father’s Kingdom.”

The Last Supper wasn’t just a meal. It was a wedding proposal. And the cross was the price of the bride.

The Mystery of the Father and the Son

In all of this, Yeshua never claimed to be the Father. Instead, He consistently distinguished Himself:

“My Father is greater than I” (John 14:28)

“I have come not to do My will, but the will of Him who sent Me” (John 6:38)

He revealed the Father, obeyed the Father, and submitted to the Father—even to the point of drinking the cup. The plan of salvation was the Father’s; the obedience unto death was the Son’s. This distinction matters because it preserves the integrity of the covenant relationship: the Son mediates the covenant, the Father receives the bride, and the Spirit seals the promise.

The Wedding Supper of the Lamb is the culmination of this story. Every covenant meal points to it. Every cup of redemption, every shared table, every act of obedience was leading here—when the Bridegroom lifts the final cup and all who are His drink it new with Him in the Kingdom of the Father.
