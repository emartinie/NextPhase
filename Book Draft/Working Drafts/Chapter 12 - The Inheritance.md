# Chapter Twelve — The Inheritance

In the previous chapter, I referred to a passage in Jeremiah about the things people inherit from their fathers.

I think about that passage not only as someone who has spent much of his life examining the things he was taught, but also as someone who has children of his own.

If I believe it is important to examine what I have inherited, then surely I have a responsibility to examine what I am passing on.

That responsibility extends far beyond the things I might someday leave my children in a will.

## The Inheritance I Never Had

For most of my adult life, I wasn't simply trying to become wealthy.

I was trying to change something about the circumstances in which the next generation of my family would begin their lives.

I didn't inherit money.

I didn't inherit land.

I didn't inherit a business that someone had spent years developing for me to take over.

Nor did I inherit a blueprint for building and preserving those things.

I had people in my life whom I loved and admired, and I don't want to diminish the importance of those relationships. But when it came to building the kind of financial foundation I wanted for my children, there was very little for me to build upon.

I knew what it felt like to have no financial safety net. I knew how easily an unexpected expense could undo the progress someone had worked so hard to make.

I wanted my children to have opportunities I hadn't had.

Not because I wanted them to live without ever having to work.

I wanted them to have something upon which their work could build.

I wanted to be the interruption in a pattern that had continued through generations.

## Every Brick Had a Purpose

As my career developed, I began to think differently about the things I acquired.

A house wasn't simply a place where we lived. It was part of the future I was trying to establish for my children.

The garden, the greenhouse, the tools, and eventually the farm and the businesses all represented things I hoped would remain available to them.

I wanted them to enjoy those things while we were together. I also wanted them to have the opportunity to build upon them someday.

My wife worked hard, too. The work she did as a homeschool mother was different from the work I did at Gibson, but it was an important part of the life our children experienced.

I wanted our home to provide stability and to be a place where our children could learn, grow, and enjoy being children.

I didn't want the only thing they inherited from me to be an understanding of how difficult it was to earn a living.

I wanted them to understand what earning a living could make possible.

I had imagined a day when I could turn over the things I had built and make myself available to help them develop those things further.

They wouldn't have to pursue exactly the same things I had pursued.

That wasn't the point.

I wanted them to have choices.

I wanted them to be able to begin somewhere other than where I had begun.

## Twelve Stones

There is a particular significance to the number twelve throughout Scripture.

Jacob had twelve sons, and the twelve tribes of Israel became central to the story of the covenant people.

Yeshua chose twelve apostles.

In Revelation 21, the New Jerusalem is described as having twelve gates bearing the names of the tribes of Israel and twelve foundations bearing the names of the apostles of the Lamb.

I find the relationship between those things particularly interesting in light of what I explained in the first chapter of this book.

But there is another biblical account involving twelve that I think is especially appropriate for this chapter.

In Joshua 4, after the children of Israel crossed the Jordan, twelve men, one from each tribe, were instructed to take twelve stones from the river.

Those stones were to become a memorial.

What interests me so much about the account is the explanation given for the memorial.

The children would ask their fathers what those stones meant.

And their fathers were to tell them what had happened.

The stones were not simply objects that one generation would leave behind for another.

They were intended to give future generations an opportunity to ask questions and to receive an account of something they had not experienced themselves.

The significance of the stones depended upon the story that accompanied them.

I think about that when I consider the things I have wanted to leave my children.

What would I want them to know about the things I built?

What would I want them to understand about why I built them?

And what would I want them to know about the God I had spent so much of my life trying to understand?

## What We Teach Our Children

In Deuteronomy 6, the instructions given to Israel concerning the words of our Creator extend into the ordinary activities of life.

They were to teach those words diligently to their children, speaking of them while sitting in their homes, walking along the way, lying down, and rising up.

I find that picture of teaching particularly meaningful.

It is not limited to a classroom or a particular hour of the week.

It is something that takes place in the course of living together.

That is one of the reasons the time I spent with my children mattered so much to me.

I wanted to provide for them, but I also wanted to be there with them.

There are things a father can teach his children through conversation. There are other things they learn by observing how he lives.

And there are things he can learn from them.

I have already explained how profoundly becoming a father changed my understanding of our Father.

My children were not simply the recipients of the things I had learned.

Their presence in my life had become part of the way I learned.

I think that is an important part of understanding what we pass from one generation to another.

## An Inheritance of Truth

Jeremiah's description of the nations acknowledging that their fathers had inherited lies presents a difficult question for anyone who is a parent.

How do we make certain that the things we pass on to our children are true?

I don't think we can accomplish that simply by being sincere.

A person can sincerely believe something that isn't true.

I have spent much of my life examining the things people believe about Scripture precisely because I recognize that possibility.

And I have to be willing to apply the same examination to the things I believe.

I don't want my children to accept something simply because Daddy said it.

I want them to understand why I believe what I believe. I want them to be willing to examine the evidence, ask questions, and acknowledge when something they have believed needs to be reconsidered.

That includes things they have learned from me.

I hope they inherit my desire to know the truth, not merely a collection of my conclusions.

I also want them to understand the importance of honesty in the way we tell our own stories.

That is part of what I am trying to accomplish in writing this book.

I cannot give them an accurate understanding of my life by telling them only the things that make me look good.

Nor would I give them an accurate understanding by allowing painful experiences to overshadow all the good things that happened.

Both belong to the life I lived.

And I have a responsibility to tell the truth about both.

## More Than Possessions

I have never stopped recognizing the value of leaving tangible resources for my children.

I don't think it is necessary to diminish the importance of material provision in order to recognize the importance of a spiritual inheritance.

Proverbs 13:22 speaks of a good man leaving an inheritance to his children's children.

I appreciate the fact that the passage directs our attention beyond even the next generation.

What we do today can have consequences for people we may never meet.

That is also part of the vision I have had for HomeGroups.

If we can help people learn things that make their homes stronger, help them develop relationships through which they can serve one another, and make useful knowledge available to their children, then the value of that work need not end with the people we initially reach.

One generation can build upon what another generation has learned.

But possessions alone cannot accomplish that.

I wanted my children to inherit courage, curiosity, integrity, and a willingness to take responsibility.

I wanted them to know how to build things, how to work with other people, and how to keep learning.

I wanted them to understand that their value wasn't determined by the things they owned or the positions they attained.

And I wanted them to know that they were loved.

Not because of what they might someday accomplish.

Because they were my children.

## What Do These Stones Mean?

I think about the question the children of Israel were expected to ask when they saw those twelve stones.

What do these stones mean?

In a sense, the first eleven chapters of this book have been my attempt to begin answering a similar question.

What did the years I spent studying Scripture mean to me?

What did my career mean?

Why did I want to build HomeGroups?

Why did I want to acquire a farm and develop businesses there?

And why was it so important to me to come home?

There are many things about my life that I have yet to explain. There are experiences I will return to, and subjects I will examine in much greater detail.

But I wanted to establish something about the foundation upon which all those things belong.

I was a man who had spent much of his life searching for truth.

And I was a father who wanted to leave something of value to his children.

I wanted them to have more than I had.

I wanted them to know more than I knew.

And I wanted them to have the freedom to build something greater than anything I had built.

The stones in Joshua were placed there so that the children would have an opportunity to ask their fathers a question.

I hope this book gives my children opportunities to ask questions of their own.

And I hope that what I leave them is not simply the story of what their father built.

I hope it helps them understand why he built it.
