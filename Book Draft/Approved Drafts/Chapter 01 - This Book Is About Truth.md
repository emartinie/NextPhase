# Chapter One — This Book Is About Truth
## Belief, covenant, and why I began building HomeGroups

*First working draft — November 2026. Written in first-person voice from conversations and earlier manuscript material. This is not an approved statement of faith or a replacement for earlier manuscripts.*

I have spent a great deal of my life asking questions that other people seemed content to leave alone.

I do not say that as a criticism of them. I have been wrong often enough to know that asking more questions does not automatically make a person wiser. Sometimes it merely gives him more opportunities to be wrong.

But I cannot remember a time when I was comfortable believing something simply because it was what I had been taught.

The question was never merely, *What does my church believe?* It was, *What is true?* And when the subject was Scripture, I wanted to know what the text said before I asked what centuries of teachers had said about it.

An old manuscript of mine began with the declaration, “This book is about Truth.” I still recognize myself in those words. I also recognize that the person who wrote them had much to learn about how truth should be pursued and how it should be shared.

I am not writing to win an argument against Christianity, Judaism, or any denomination. I am writing because I believe the biblical story is more connected than many of us have been taught to see. I believe its covenants matter, its names matter, its distinctions matter, and the identity of the people to whom its promises were made matters.

I also believe that if I am mistaken, I ought to want to know.

That is not a decorative disclaimer. It is part of the undertaking.

### The book I thought I knew

I grew up encountering the Bible through familiar divisions: Old Testament and New Testament, Israel and the Church, law and grace, Jews and Gentiles. Those categories can be useful descriptions. They can also become walls. If a reader accepts the walls before reading the story, the story begins to look like two different religious systems: an old one that failed and a new one that replaced it.

I no longer read it that way.

I read from Genesis forward, as one unfolding account of creation, human rebellion, promise, covenant, exile, judgment, mercy, and restoration. The writings of the apostles are not, to my mind, a declaration that the earlier Scriptures have become irrelevant. They are testimony concerning the Messiah of those Scriptures.

That is why I often use the name Yeshua alongside Jesus. I am not suggesting that a person must pronounce a particular name to know him. I am trying to remember that the man at the center of the Gospels belonged to a particular people, spoke within a particular world, and was understood by his earliest followers in relation to Israel's Scriptures.

I believe Yeshua is the promised Messiah, the Word and image of God, and the one through whom the Father accomplishes redemption. I do not confuse the Father with the Son. I want to take seriously both their relationship and the language Scripture uses about them, rather than forcing every passage into a slogan.

The same concern shapes how I approach the Holy Spirit, the kingdom, resurrection, judgment, and the hope of new creation. These are not isolated subjects to me. They belong to the same story.

### Why the Torah would not go away

The first five books of Scripture became foundational to my understanding, not merely because they appear first, but because so much of what follows depends on them.

Genesis gives us origins: humanity, nations, promise, the beginnings of Israel, and the relationships that shape the rest of the narrative. Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy describe redemption, covenant, holiness, worship, justice, and the obligations of a people called to belong to the Creator.

I believe the Torah is holy, righteous, and good. I do not believe Yeshua came to make obedience to the Father meaningless. I do not believe the apostles taught that sin ceased to be sin because Messiah came.

There was a time when I understood the prophetic significance of the appointed times and how they pointed to Messiah without understanding my own responsibility to observe them.

What changed was not simply my understanding of the commandments. It was my understanding of who I was.

As I came to understand what it meant to be a new creation in Messiah, grafted into Israel and made a fellow citizen of the Commonwealth of Israel, I could no longer separate my theology from my identity.

I did not adopt a set of practices in pursuit of a new identity. My practices changed because my understanding of my identity changed.

I did not begin observing the appointed times in order to become Israel. I began observing them because I had come to understand myself as Israel through Messiah.

Obedience, therefore, is not the means by which I acquire my inheritance. It is an expression of my recognition of the inheritance I have received.

That distinction is foundational to everything that follows.

That conviction leads me to take the Sabbath, the appointed times, the dietary instructions, and the commandments seriously. I observe the Sabbath and understand Passover and the other appointed times as part of the biblical story, not embarrassing relics to be discarded. I also recognize that sincere believers disagree about how particular commandments apply today, especially in the absence of a Temple and within the realities of life among the nations.

The point is not that I have perfectly kept every commandment. I have not. Nor do I think outward observance alone proves a faithful heart.

The prophets repeatedly confront people who perform religious acts while neglecting justice, mercy, faithfulness, and the vulnerable. Yeshua does the same. Obedience without love is not the goal. Neither is a profession of love that refuses to listen to the one it claims to love.

To me, a *mitzvah* is not merely a rule on a checklist. A commandment can be an invitation into relationship: the beloved listening to the voice of the one who loves him. I want the heart and the act together.

My choice to use the widely observed Hillel calendar for shared appointed-time gatherings illustrates a tension I have learned to live with. I know the calendar raises historical and textual questions. But gathering with other people also matters. I do not want the pursuit of precision to become an excuse for never assembling.

### Two houses, one story

Of all the themes that have reshaped my reading, few have been as consequential as the distinction between the House of Judah and the House of Israel.

The united kingdom divided. Judah and the northern kingdom did not thereafter have identical histories. The prophets address them distinctly, even while promising restoration.

I believe this distinction is often blurred when every reference to “Israel,” “the Jews,” and “God's people” is treated as interchangeable.

In my reading, the northern House of Israel was described as divorced and scattered, while Judah's history and covenantal standing were not identical. Jeremiah 31 names the House of Israel and the House of Judah in the promise of a new covenant. Ezekiel 37 portrays two sticks becoming one in God's hand. Hosea's language of estrangement and mercy, the prophetic promises of return, and the apostles' treatment of the nations have become central to my understanding.

I believe Yeshua's mission must be read in the light of that restoration. His statement about the lost sheep of the House of Israel matters to me. So does the disciples' question in Acts 1 about restoring the kingdom to Israel. So do Paul's olive-tree imagery in Romans 11 and his language of citizenship and belonging in Ephesians 2.

I have come to understand myself as someone brought near through Messiah, grafted into Israel's covenantal story rather than given a disconnected religious identity. I have sometimes described that identity in Ephraimite terms, though I do not claim to have proven a biological genealogy.

This is the heart of what is sometimes called *two-house restoration*. It is a conviction I hold strongly. It is also an interpretation that deserves careful examination, not an assumption every reader must accept before turning the page.

The text does not become stronger because I refuse to admit that others read it differently. The opposite is true. If the case is sound, it should withstand comparison with serious alternative readings.

### The nations and the covenants

I have said something in conversation that can sound startling: I do not find a covenant made *with Gentiles as Gentiles* in the way the new covenant is explicitly promised to the two houses in Jeremiah.

That is not my way of saying that people from the nations are excluded. It is almost the opposite. I understand their inclusion through the Messiah and the promises of Israel. In my reading, those who were far off are brought near; they become fellow citizens rather than permanent outsiders.

This is one of the places where words matter enormously. “Gentile” can describe a person from the nations, a former condition of estrangement, or a social distinction in a particular passage. It should not be made to mean the same thing in every context without investigation.

Nor do I think the Church should be presented as a replacement people that renders the promises to Israel void. God's faithfulness to His word is too central to the story for that.

I recognize that covenant theologians, dispensationalists, Messianic Jews, scholars of Paul, and other readers will challenge parts of this account. They should. I want to hear their strongest readings, not caricatures.

### Paul, James, and the language of inherited tradition

The more I study the apostles, the less satisfied I am with the notion that Paul founded a religion opposed to the Torah.

I understand Paul as a Jewish apostle wrestling with the inclusion of the nations, the significance of Messiah, and the meaning of faithfulness within Israel's story. His letters are difficult. Peter himself acknowledges that difficulty. But I do not believe the safest solution is to make Paul contradict Moses, the prophets, Yeshua, and himself.

Names offer a small but revealing example of how tradition can become invisible to us. The figure English Bibles call James is Yaakov—Jacob. I have spent time asking how a reader's sense of continuity changes when familiar people are encountered by their Hebrew names. The point is not to shame translators or readers. It is to notice how easily inherited language shapes the story we think we know.

The same method sends me back to the Hebrew and Greek, to textual variants, ancient Jewish literature, geography, archaeology, and the social world of the first century.

During years of incarceration I devoted extraordinary time to studying Scripture and its languages. That period did not make me infallible. It made me aware of how much I did not know, and it gave me the opportunity to pursue questions that would otherwise have been crowded out.

I have read beyond the Protestant canon, including Enoch, Jubilees, and other ancient writings. Those books have influenced questions I ask, but influence is not the same thing as assigning every work the same authority as Torah or the canonical Scriptures. Historical value, interpretive value, and scriptural authority must be considered separately.

### What I believe about truth and evidence

Some patterns in Scripture strike me as so intricate that I see in the Torah a fingerprint of intelligence beyond its human setting. That is my conviction, not a mathematical proof I can impose on another person.

I have explored chronology, prophecy, symbolic periods, the restoration of Israel, the creation account, and proposed correspondences between biblical texts. Some of these studies remain hypotheses. A pattern that excites me is not automatically an established fact. Numerical constructions, historical reconstructions, and prophetic applications must be tested with particular care.

I believe the Creator reveals Himself progressively, but I do not mean that later revelation makes earlier revelation false. I mean that the story develops, promises unfold, and human understanding can grow. Sometimes what people call a new discovery is actually a return to something long neglected.

Science does not frighten me. Neither do archaeology, history, or textual criticism. I want to distinguish what the evidence demonstrates, what it suggests, and what I believe on theological grounds.

I have been wrong. I expect to discover more places where I am wrong. The aim of study is not to make my opinions unchangeable. It is to become more faithful to what is true.

### Why I am writing

I am writing first because I want my children to understand what their father spent so much of his life trying to understand.

I do not need them to agree with every conclusion. I want them to know that I asked the questions sincerely, that I was willing to examine difficult evidence, and that the search was never supposed to become an excuse to love people less.

I am also writing for readers who have sensed that familiar explanations leave parts of the biblical story disconnected. I want to invite them to read again: to follow the covenant language, to distinguish the houses, to hear the prophets in the words of the apostles, and to examine whether the Messiah's mission is even more coherent than they had imagined.

This book is not intended to be a prosecution of Christians who celebrate differently, worship on different days, or inherit different traditions. I have strong convictions about worship and the appointed times. I do not want those convictions turned into contempt for people. A family gathering is not an opportunity I am entitled to seize for an argument. People deserve love without having to pass a theological examination.

If the reader finishes this book disagreeing with me but more willing to study Scripture carefully and love other people faithfully, something worthwhile will have happened.

### Why I began building HomeGroups

A book can preserve an argument. An application can make the investigation participatory.

That distinction gradually became important to me.

I wanted readers to be able to open a passage, compare translations, inspect words, follow cross-references, hear the text, see places on a map, examine the calendar, and ask questions with other people. I wanted the Torah, Prophets, Writings, Gospels, and apostolic letters to be read in conversation rather than in isolation.

And I wanted the study to lead somewhere beyond study.

The mission of HomeGroups can be said simply: study the entire Bible together; test everything; love one another; equip disciples who make disciples.

The technology is not the mission. It should not replace a family, a friendship, a congregation, a pastor, or the Holy Spirit. If it helps people learn while making them less capable of speaking to one another, it has missed its purpose.

That is why Fellowship became so important to the project. A person ought to have a place to begin with people they trust. I have come to believe that every participant should begin with a private Family space, even if that person is initially its only member. And I do not believe software should decide who counts as family. Biology does not automatically create trust, and affection does not require shared ancestry. Each person should decide whom to invite.

That is a design decision, but it is also a statement about human dignity.

I have also begun asking harder architectural questions. What remains of a study tool when the internet disappears? What remains of a community application when its communication service fails? The answers are not yet complete. I do not want to pretend the application has already fulfilled all its ambitions, or that a working demonstration is the same thing as a living community.

At the time of this writing, much of the Fellowship environment is still moving from test data toward real participation. I am building the place before I can know all the people who may someday gather there.

That uncertainty is not a reason to abandon the work. It is a reason to be honest about its stage.

### The kind of invitation I want to make

There was a time when I imagined that discovering the truth meant I should be able to dismantle every mistaken tradition and persuade everyone who would listen. I can still recognize the urgency behind that impulse. I no longer think urgency gives me permission to be harsh.

People are not projects to be corrected. They are people to be loved.

I believe the Scriptures tell a story of a faithful Creator, a people divided and promised restoration, a Messiah who brings the distant near, and a kingdom in which obedience and love belong together.

I believe the covenant story is still alive.

I believe truth can withstand honest questions.

I believe fellowship must become more than a word on a screen.

And I believe that whatever I build, write, or teach should help another person draw nearer to the Creator and nearer to other people—not nearer to my importance.

So I will begin this book where my search began: with questions, with Scripture, and with the willingness to be corrected.

This book is about truth.

It is also about what truth ought to do to the person who seeks it.

When I look back across the years, I don't see a straight line.

I see questions.

I see victories.

I see failures.

I see seasons that made no sense while I was living them but now fit together in ways I never could have imagined.

If I could speak to the young man carrying that notebook full of questions, I don't think I would give him many answers.

Instead, I would tell him to keep asking.

### The Journey Was the Teacher

There were times I believed I had finally figured everything out.

Then life would humble me.

A new experience.

A painful loss.

A relationship.

A failure.

A discovery hidden in Scripture.

Each one reminded me that truth is too large to be possessed.

It must be pursued.

The older I become, the less interested I am in winning arguments.

I'm far more interested in understanding people, listening carefully, and allowing evidence to reshape my thinking whenever necessary.

That isn't weakness;

It's growth.

### What Remains

Careers change.

Businesses rise and fall.

Dreams evolve.

People come and go.

But some things endure.

Faith.

Hope.

Love.

Purpose.

The conviction that every life has value.

The belief that redemption is possible.

The certainty that God is still writing stories long after we believe the chapter has ended.

Those are the things I carry with me today.

### The Search Continues

People sometimes ask whether I have finally found the truth.

My answer surprises them.

Yes.

And no.

I know the One who called Himself the Truth.

But knowing Him hasn't ended my search.

It has transformed it.

Now I don't study to prove myself right or others wrong.

I study because wonder has replaced certainty.

Curiosity has replaced fear.

The search continues—not only because truth is often hidden, but because it is inexhaustible.

Every page of Scripture.

Every conversation.

Every hardship.

Every blessing.

Each reveals another glimpse of the Creator.

### A Final Thought

If this book has one message, I hope it is this:

Don't be afraid of difficult questions.

Don't be afraid to admit when you're wrong.

Don't be afraid to change my mind when the evidence leads somewhere unexpected.

Truth has nothing to fear from honest investigation.

And neither should we.

The journey isn't over.

In many ways...

It's only just beginning.

There are seasons in life when you become convinced that some chapters have ended forever.

I don't remember deciding to search for truth.

I only remember realizing I couldn't stop.

When I was seventeen, I started carrying a notebook with me wherever I went. It wasn't filled with poetry or stories. It was filled with questions.

Questions about God.

Questions about life.

Questions about why the world could be so beautiful one moment and so broken the next.

Looking back, I realize that notebook became the beginning of a lifelong journey.

The Early Search
At first, I assumed truth would be easy to find.

Surely someone already had the answers.

Surely there was one teacher, one book, one church, one philosophy that could explain everything.

Instead, every answer seemed to produce three more questions.

Rather than discouraging me, it pulled me deeper.

I read Scripture.

I read philosophy.

I read science.

I listened more than I spoke.

People sometimes mistook my questions for rebellion.

They weren't.

I wasn't trying to tear anything down.

I was trying to understand.

Faith and Thinking
One thing became clear very early in my life.

I wasn't interested in blind faith.

Neither was I interested in knowledge without purpose.

I wanted both.

A faith that welcomed difficult questions.

A mind that wasn't afraid of mystery.

For years one quotation stayed in the back of my mind:

"An educated man should know something about everything, and everything about something."

Whether I knew its source or not, it became a personal compass.

It reminded me that learning wasn't about collecting information.

It was about becoming a more complete human being.

The Journey
That search eventually carried me into places I never expected.

Prison.

Churches.

Corporate boardrooms.

Technology.

Farming.

Business.

Friendships.

Failure.

Success.

Every season taught me something the previous one could not.

Truth wasn't confined to one building, one profession, or one chapter of life.

It revealed itself in unexpected places.

The search for truth eventually brought me face to face with the most difficult subject of all:

People.

Not governments.

Not science.

Not religion.

People.

Including myself.

The longer I lived, the more I realized that humanity is both magnificent and deeply broken. We are capable of incredible compassion and unimaginable cruelty—sometimes within the same day.Because we're all just broken people.

The Bible calls this condition sin.

Psychology often calls it brokenness.

Life simply calls it reality.

The Battle Within
I saw this most clearly during my time in prison.

I met men who had committed terrible crimes, yet who still possessed kindness, generosity, and a desire to become something better.

I also met officers whose responsibility was to enforce order while quietly carrying burdens of their own and treating inmates terribly.

Those experiences changed the way I viewed people.

The line between good and evil wasn't as simple as I once imagined.

Every one of us carries an internal struggle. Years later I was introduced to something called the yetzer Hara- the inclination for evil and the inclination for good.

The desire to do what is right.

The temptation to choose what is easier.

The longing to become better.

The fear that we never will.

Recognizing that struggle didn't excuse anyone's choices, but it did help me develop compassion.

It became harder to judge others harshly once I began to recognize the same weaknesses within myself. But it was much easier for me to judge myself.

Why Change Is So Difficult
Over the years I became convinced that lasting change cannot be manufactured through rules alone.

Rules have their place.

Education has tremendous value.

Discipline matters.

But none of those things can transform a human heart by themselves.

That realization influenced nearly everything I later tried to build, including Project Uplift.

I believed then—and still believe now—that people need more than information.

They need hope.

They need purpose.

They need grace.

Without those, rehabilitation often becomes little more than behavior management.

Redemption
This is why redemption became such an important theme in my life.

We are not simply people who need better habits.

We are people who need renewal.

The Christian message has always resonated with me because it doesn't begin by pretending people are already whole.

It begins by acknowledging our brokenness.

Only then does it point toward healing.

Only then does grace become meaningful.

Reflection
Understanding the human condition changed the way I viewed everyone around me.

It made me slower to condemn.

Quicker to listen.

More willing to believe that people can change.

Because if redemption is possible for one person...

Then it remains possible for all of us.
