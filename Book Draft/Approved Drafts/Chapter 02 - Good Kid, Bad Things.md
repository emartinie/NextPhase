# Chapter Two — Good Kid, Bad Things

*First working draft — November 2026. Written in first-person voice from original “Good kid, bad things…” list and conversations. Narrative order is thematic, not a verified chronology. Details remain subject to review.*

I have sometimes wondered how different my life might have been if the people around me had lived different lives.

That is a difficult question, because it assumes there was another version of my childhood waiting somewhere, one in which the adults made better decisions, the people I loved were protected, and the things that happened behind closed doors never happened at all.

I don't know that version of my life.

I know the one I lived.

Years ago, I sat down and made a list. I called it *Good Kid, Bad Things*. It wasn't particularly well written. It was mostly a collection of memories, events, and turning points. Some were terrible. Some were wonderful. Some were things that happened to me, and others were things I did that I would eventually have to take responsibility for.

Looking at that list today, I realize how much it leaves out.

But I also recognize the person who wrote it.

I think he was trying to understand something that had troubled him for much of his life: how a child who wasn't born wanting to hurt anyone could eventually find himself doing things he never imagined he would do.

And perhaps, more importantly, how that same person could eventually become someone else.

My mother's life was difficult long before I was old enough to understand it. She had suffered terrible abuse. Alcohol became part of her story, as did troubled relationships, infidelity, and the tremendous responsibility of raising children while trying to survive herself.

I loved my mother. I still do.

But loving someone does not require pretending that the things they did, or the things that happened around them, didn't affect you.

There were things in my family that children should never have had to experience or know about. Sexual abuse, violence, betrayal, and secrets that nobody seemed willing to discuss. I remember the helplessness of knowing something was terribly wrong and having no idea what I could possibly do about it.

Silence was sometimes as much a part of the experience as the event itself.

My biological father disappeared from my life. Other men came and went. Some brought instability and fear. Others gave me glimpses of what a father figure could be.

My grandfather and my uncle Les were heroes to me. I even thought of them as superheroes. They were men I could look toward when so much else seemed uncertain.

I sometimes wonder whether they ever realized how important they were.

Families are complicated. A child can love people who disappoint him, admire people who are imperfect, and feel abandoned by people who may have had struggles he couldn't possibly understand.

As I grew older, the instability continued. We moved. Relationships changed. People died. People hurt one another. Some of the people closest to me made decisions that altered their lives forever.

And somewhere along the way, I began making decisions of my own.

I ran away from home. More than once.

I stopped going to school. I started drinking and smoking. I became involved with people and activities that would eventually lead me into increasingly serious trouble.

There were gangs, drugs, homelessness, crime, and jail.

I could spend a great deal of time explaining the circumstances surrounding each of those things. Some of those explanations would be important. Others might sound suspiciously like excuses.

I don't want to confuse the two.

There were things I could not control as a child. There were also things I chose to do, particularly as I grew older, for which I was responsible.

Both statements are true.

I think that's one of the difficulties people encounter when they try to understand their own lives. We want to identify the moment when everything went wrong, as though there must have been one event, one person, or one decision responsible for everything that followed.

But lives don't generally work that way.

Mine certainly didn't.

I also remember that there were good things happening in the middle of the bad ones. I discovered skateboarding. I developed friendships. I found interests that gave me something to pursue beyond whatever trouble surrounded me.

And eventually, I bought a guitar.

I wrote four words about that in my original list:

*Bought a guitar. Life changed.*

Those four words deserve considerably more attention than I gave them.

Music gave me something I had been missing. It gave me a reason to practice, to improve, to create, and to imagine a future that wasn't defined entirely by my past.

I earned my GED. I started college. I formed a band.

For a while, music became one of the most important things in my life.

Then one of my closest friends died by suicide.

I couldn't sing and play anymore.

I withdrew. I didn't return. I didn't even attend his funeral, although someone had purchased a plane ticket for me.

There are experiences that change the direction of a life, and there are experiences that seem to bring it to a complete stop.

That was one of them.

I wish I could say that every tragedy taught me something immediately, that each painful experience made me wiser, or that I always responded to suffering by becoming a better person.

I can't.

Sometimes I responded terribly.

Eventually, I found myself in prison.

I spent approximately three years incarcerated following a drug case. By then, my life had traveled a considerable distance from the childhood I had once known.

And yet, something happened during those years that would profoundly change the direction of everything that followed.

I began studying Scripture.

Not casually. Not occasionally. I had time, and I used an extraordinary amount of it reading, examining, comparing, and questioning the biblical text.

For years, I studied for ten hours a day or more.

I became interested in the original languages, in the historical settings of the Scriptures, in the relationships between passages, and in the questions that arose whenever something I read seemed inconsistent with what I had been taught.

I had lost my guitar.

But I had found Jesus.

That was how I described it in my original list, and I still recognize the significance of those words.

I don't mean that prison was somehow a good thing simply because something good emerged from it. I don't believe suffering must be justified by whatever lessons we eventually learn.

But I cannot deny what happened there.

The questions that would eventually shape my understanding of Scripture, covenant, Israel, Messiah, and restoration began to occupy my attention with an intensity I had never experienced before.

The boy who had once felt powerless in the face of things he couldn't understand had become a man determined to understand something.

And this time, the questions were about God.

My life didn't suddenly become simple when I left prison.

I built businesses. I developed a career. I married. I became a father. Eventually, I spent many years working at Gibson, a company whose very name would once have meant something extraordinary to the young man who had bought his first guitar.

There were successes I never would have imagined during some of the darkest years of my life.

There were also losses.

My marriage deteriorated. I experienced abandonment and betrayal. There were periods when my relationship with my children was disrupted in ways that were deeply painful.

I had spent much of my childhood trying to understand why families broke apart.

Now I was watching my own family struggle.

And I had children who would experience the consequences of decisions they had never made.

That realization carries a particular weight.

I know what it feels like to be a child surrounded by adult problems. I know how confusing it can be to love people whose decisions hurt one another. I know that children remember things adults sometimes assume they will forget.

I cannot rewrite my childhood.

I cannot undo the mistakes I made as a young man.

I cannot go backward and change every decision that affected my children.

But I can decide what I do with the life I have now.

Perhaps that is one of the reasons truth became so important to me.

Not merely theological truth, although that has occupied an enormous part of my life. I mean the willingness to acknowledge what happened, to examine my own responsibility, and to resist the temptation to make the story more comfortable than it really was.

There are things about my life I am proud of.

There are things I regret.

There are people I loved who hurt me, and people I hurt who loved me.

There are questions I still cannot answer.

And there are experiences I haven't yet found the words to describe.

When I look back at that old list, I don't see a complete autobiography. I see fragments of a life that I was still trying to understand.

Somewhere in those fragments is a little boy who wanted to be loved, who needed people he could trust, and who didn't understand why so many terrible things happened to people he cared about.

I don't think that boy disappeared.

I think he grew up.

He made mistakes. Some were serious. He suffered losses, found purpose, became a father, and continued asking questions.

And somewhere along the way, he began to understand that finding truth and living truth are not necessarily the same thing.

That distinction matters to me now more than ever.

I have spent years studying what the Scriptures say about the Creator, His commandments, His covenants, and His promises.

But I also have to ask what those convictions mean in the way I treat the people I love.

Especially my children.

Looking back, I can see another connection running through that old list. So much of what shaped my life involved people who needed someone to stand beside them. Families struggling, children left without protection, people suffering in silence, and relationships that could not withstand the weight placed upon them.

I had wanted something different for much of my life. People gathered together, learning from one another, caring for one another, and sharing responsibility for the well-being of those around them.

I didn't always have the language to describe that desire. Eventually, I would call it HomeGroups.

The name came much later than the need.

I cannot give my children a perfect father or a perfect explanation of everything that has happened.

I can give them the truth as best I understand it, the willingness to acknowledge when I have been wrong, and the assurance that my love for them does not depend on their agreement with me.

Perhaps that's part of what I was searching for all along.

When I first wrote *Good Kid, Bad Things*, I was trying to make sense of the events that had shaped my life.

Today, I find myself asking a slightly different question.

Not simply what happened to me, or even why.

But what kind of man those experiences have helped me become—and what kind of man I still have the opportunity to be.
