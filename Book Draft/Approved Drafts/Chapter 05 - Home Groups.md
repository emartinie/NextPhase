# Chapter Five — Home Groups

I never stopped studying the Bible.

And I never stopped believing that communities of people taking care of one another were foundational to the life Scripture described.

Whether I was attending a church or not, that conviction remained.

It wasn't something that depended upon my membership in a congregation, my employment, or the circumstances of my life. It was part of my understanding of Scripture and what it meant to live according to the instructions of our Creator.

But as the demands of my career and my responsibilities at home increased, finding opportunities to participate in that kind of community became increasingly difficult.

So I began relying more heavily on something I understood very well.

Technology.

## People Behind the Technology

At Gibson, I had hundreds of people whose lives I could make a little easier.

Every problem I solved represented an opportunity to help someone. And while I was helping them, I had opportunities to talk with them, get to know them, and develop relationships.

I was working for a global company, but that company was built upon people.

Behind every computer, every application, every system, and every technical problem was a human being trying to accomplish something.

My responsibility was to help make that possible.

Technology was the means through which I served them.

And that distinction is important because it would eventually become central to what I wanted to accomplish with Home Groups.

I wasn't interested in technology merely for the sake of technology.

I was interested in what it enabled people to do for one another.

## Before Facebook

Long before I began working at Gibson, I had been building websites and experimenting with applications.

I never stopped doing that.

Just as I never stopped studying Scripture, I never stopped exploring ways that technology could help accomplish some of the things I believed Scripture was calling us to do.

I had a website.

And I never gave up on having a website of my own.

But there are enormous limitations to what you can accomplish with a website, particularly when you haven't really told anyone about it.

You can build something meaningful. You can spend countless hours developing it. You can fill it with information that you believe could benefit other people.

But none of that guarantees that anyone will ever see it.

And even if people do see it, that doesn't necessarily mean they will develop relationships with one another.

Having a place where people can gather is not the same thing as having a community.

A website could provide the place.

What I still needed was a way to bring the people together.

## Discovering Facebook

In 2008, I reluctantly joined Facebook.

I didn't join because I was particularly interested in social media. I joined because I was becoming increasingly concerned that the future I had been working so hard to build for my children might be in trouble.

And when I got there, I discovered something I hadn't expected.

Almost everybody I knew was already there.

I didn't initially use Facebook the way many other people seemed to use it.

I wasn't particularly interested in what people were having for dinner, what their hobbies were, or what they had done over the weekend.

I didn't spend my time looking at people's profiles or reading their posts.

I was there to share information.

I began posting news articles and sharing facts about things I believed most people weren't aware of. I wanted people to know about things that I believed were important, particularly things that could affect the future we were building for our children.

For a while, that was essentially the extent of my relationship with Facebook.

It was a means of communicating information to people I already knew.

But then Facebook improved its Groups functionality.

And suddenly, I saw an entirely different possibility.

## Bringing People Together

Facebook presented both an opportunity and a problem.

Because of my position at Gibson, there were hundreds of people who knew me professionally. There were things I wanted to discuss that I didn't necessarily want to introduce into my professional relationships.

I needed a way to have those conversations without having every conversation become a public conversation.

So I began creating secret groups.

I could now bring people together in environments where we could discuss particular subjects without sharing everything with everyone.

And unlike my own website, Facebook already had the people.

I didn't have to convince everyone to visit a website they had never heard of. I didn't have to convince them to establish another account or learn how to use another application.

They were already there.

That was an enormous advantage.

But the opportunity I saw wasn't simply the ability to distribute more information.

It was the ability to begin developing communities.

There is a significant difference between communicating something to a hundred people and connecting a hundred people to one another.

I had been doing the former.

Now I had an opportunity to pursue the latter.

And everything I wanted to accomplish through those communities was foundationally Scripture-based.

## What Scripture Is About

When I say that something is Scripture-based, I don't mean that every conversation must revolve around interpreting a particular passage of the Bible.

Scripture is about life.

It is about how we relate to our Creator as His creations and how we relate to one another.

It is about families, communities, responsibilities, justice, mercy, and love.

It is about how we treat the people around us.

It is about what we do when someone is hungry, when someone is suffering, when someone needs protection, or when someone simply needs another human being to stand beside them.

These are not subjects that exist independently of Scripture.

They are among the very things Scripture teaches us.

What good is studying the commandments concerning our neighbors if we never develop relationships with our neighbors?

What good is studying our responsibility to care for those in need if we never establish relationships through which we can recognize those needs?

What good is understanding what love means if we never create opportunities to express it?

I wasn't interested in separating the study of Scripture from the practice of Scripture.

I wanted to bring people together so that we could learn together, grow together, and take care of one another.

Studying Scripture was foundational to that objective.

But the objective wasn't merely to accumulate knowledge.

It was to live differently because of what we had learned.

## The Importance of Community

If you have read the preceding chapters, perhaps you can begin to understand why these things mattered so much to me.

I knew what it was like to be a child who needed protection.

I knew what it was like to need a home.

I knew what it was like to encounter circumstances that a person should not have to face alone.

And when I became a father, I devoted myself to giving my children many of the things I had needed during my own childhood.

But my children weren't the only children in the world.

My family wasn't the only family in the world.

There were other people who needed many of the same things I had needed, and other families trying to provide many of the same things I was trying to provide.

No single family could solve every problem.

No single individual could meet every need.

But communities of people who knew one another, cared about one another, and accepted some responsibility for one another could accomplish things that individuals struggling independently could not.

I believed that before I ever built a website.

I believed it before I ever worked at Gibson.

I believed it before I ever joined Facebook.

And I believed it whether I was attending a church or not.

It was never dependent upon the technology available to me.

Technology simply gave me additional opportunities to act upon that belief.

## Home Groups

The words *Home Groups* represent something much greater to me than the name of a website or an application.

They represent an understanding of how people can live together.

The home is where so much of our lives takes place. It is where children are raised, where families develop relationships, and where many of our greatest joys and greatest struggles are experienced.

But I don't believe our responsibility to one another was ever intended to end at the boundaries of our own homes.

What if the concern we have for the people inside our homes could extend beyond them?

What if families knew other families well enough to recognize when someone needed help?

What if studying Scripture together naturally led to doing things together?

What if our knowledge of one another was sufficient to make our expressions of love more than words?

These were the kinds of things I wanted to accomplish.

And throughout my life, I continued looking for ways to accomplish them.

I had my own website. I experimented with applications. I began using Facebook. I created groups.

Each of those things represented another opportunity to pursue the same objective.

The tools changed.

The opportunities changed.

The circumstances of my life changed.

But the foundation remained the same.

Scripture was the foundation.

Fellowship was the connection.

And helping one another was where what we believed began to find expression in the lives we lived.

The technology was new.

The foundation was ancient.
