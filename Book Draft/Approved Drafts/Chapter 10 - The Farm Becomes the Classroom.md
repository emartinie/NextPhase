# Chapter Ten — The Farm Becomes the Classroom

Buying Big Whiskers Farm gave me something I had never had before.

I had a place where many of the things I had been studying, teaching, and developing over the years could become part of the same environment.

But I had also acquired an existing business. There were things already happening at Big Whiskers that had nothing to do with the plans I had for it.

I had to learn how to work with what was already there.

In a sense, I was being presented with the same opportunity I had encouraged other people to recognize years earlier on my HomeGroups website.

Start where you are with what you have.

## Working With What Was There

Big Whiskers was known for its catfish ponds and fishing tournaments. People already had reasons to come there, and the business had developed around those activities.

That presented me with some interesting questions as a believer.

By that time in my life, I had become convinced that the Sabbath was something our Creator intended His people to observe. I understood it to extend from Friday evening to Saturday evening.

Saturday was also the day on which the farm held its fishing tournaments.

And according to my understanding of the dietary instructions in Scripture, neither catfish nor pork was food. Yet both were part of the business I had acquired.

I think it is important to acknowledge those things because they illustrate something about the difficulty of putting our beliefs into practice.

It is one thing to study a passage of Scripture and arrive at a conviction about what it means.

It is another thing to determine how to live according to that conviction when you find yourself responsible for something that has been operating quite differently.

I had acquired a business with established practices. I couldn't pretend that those practices were consistent with everything I believed. Nor did I think that the only way forward was to eliminate everything overnight.

I understood my responsibility to be one of stewardship and transformation.

I wanted to develop the property in a direction that was increasingly consistent with my understanding of Scripture while recognizing the circumstances in which I had found myself.

There was a great deal of work to do.

And there was a great deal to learn.

## The Things We Could Grow

One of the things that had originally brought Travis into my life was my interest in aquaponics.

At Big Whiskers, I had an opportunity to pursue that interest on an entirely different scale.

I was interested in the relationship between aquaculture, hydroponics, and aquaponics. Each offered different possibilities for producing food and managing resources.

Hydroponics involves growing plants without relying on soil as the growing medium. Aquaculture involves raising aquatic organisms. Aquaponics brings aspects of the two together, using the relationships between fish, plants, and microorganisms to support a growing system.

What interested me wasn't simply the ability to grow things differently.

I wanted to understand how these systems worked, what could be accomplished with them, and how that knowledge could be made useful to other people.

The farm included a fifty-thousand-gallon fish tank in the ground. I developed indoor growing environments and a showroom where different approaches to cultivation could be explored.

I also designed home aquaponics units that were manufactured by a friend and mentor in China.

Think about how different that sounds from the work I had been doing at Gibson.

Yet many of the things I had learned throughout my career were still applicable.

I was working with systems.

I was examining relationships between different components, considering how they affected one another, and looking for ways to make the whole system function more effectively.

And I was still working with people.

The experience and relationships I had acquired through technology and international business didn't become irrelevant when I began working in agriculture.

They became resources I could apply to something else.

## Bringing Knowledge Home

Not everyone who came to Big Whiskers was going to acquire a farm.

And not everyone needed to.

I had started experimenting with these things in my own backyard. I understood that people could begin learning about food production and sustainable living without having access to the kinds of resources I now had available.

That was one of the reasons the home aquaponics units interested me.

What could someone learn from a smaller system that they could operate themselves?

What could a family discover about the relationship between the things our Creator made simply by observing how those things worked together?

There is value in reading about something. There is value in watching someone explain it. But there is also value in having an opportunity to work with it yourself.

I wanted the farm to provide opportunities for people to do all three.

And I wanted what people learned there to have value when they returned to their own homes.

That was consistent with what I had been trying to accomplish through HomeGroups all along.

The resources weren't supposed to end with the person who received them.

They were supposed to become useful in that person's life.

## Preparedness in Practice

The emergency preparedness resources I had accumulated over the years also became part of what I was doing at the farm.

I had already made enormous amounts of information available through my website. I had taught people about preparedness through groups and had developed relationships with people who shared those interests.

Now there was a physical place where we could gather and discuss some of those subjects.

I hosted community meetings and taught practical preparedness skills, including amateur radio and emergency communications.

I was particularly interested in helping people understand that communications equipment was only one part of developing the ability to communicate.

A radio doesn't establish a relationship between two people.

It provides a means by which people can communicate.

There is a considerable difference between owning equipment and knowing how to use it, just as there is a difference between knowing how to use it and having people with whom you can use it.

That is why I believed developing those relationships was part of preparedness itself.

We could learn about communications before we needed them in an emergency. We could become familiar with the knowledge and capabilities of other people in our communities. We could help one another become better equipped.

And we could do those things without having to live in fear of what might happen tomorrow.

The farm gave us an environment in which to begin putting some of those ideas into practice.

## Saturday at Big Whiskers

Some of the most meaningful experiences at the farm had very little to do with the technology I was developing or the things I was teaching.

They happened around the ponds.

On Saturdays, the fishing tournaments could bring a hundred people together. Children, parents, grandparents, and people with different abilities could all participate in the life of the place.

I enjoyed seeing families spending time together.

I enjoyed seeing children experience the excitement of catching fish.

And I appreciated that the farm provided opportunities for people who might otherwise have had very few reasons to encounter one another.

There is something about spending time together in an ordinary environment that can make conversation possible in a way that a more formal setting sometimes cannot.

People can talk while they fish.

They can talk while they eat.

They can talk about their lives without having to arrange a meeting specifically for that purpose.

I had spent years thinking about the importance of people gathering in homes. What I saw at Big Whiskers helped illustrate that the relationships we develop through those gatherings don't have to be confined to the walls of a house.

The same people who study Scripture together can work together, learn practical skills together, and enjoy spending time together.

Fellowship can be part of the ordinary things we do.

## A Classroom Without Walls

I have often thought about the way Yeshua taught.

He taught in synagogues, but He also taught in homes, beside the water, and in the course of traveling with His disciples.

He drew upon things people could recognize from the lives they lived: seeds, soil, fishing, bread, vineyards, and the responsibilities of caring for animals.

Those familiar things became opportunities to communicate truths of much greater significance.

I don't think we need to manufacture a spiritual lesson out of every activity in which we participate.

But I do think we should recognize how many opportunities to teach and learn arise naturally when we spend time with one another.

A person who might never attend a formal Bible study may be perfectly comfortable asking a question about Scripture in the course of a conversation beside a pond.

Someone who has knowledge about growing food may have an opportunity to share it with a person who has never grown anything.

And the person doing the teaching in one conversation may become the person doing the learning in the next.

I had experienced that myself when I met Travis.

The farm gave me opportunities to experience more of that kind of fellowship.

## Beyond Big Whiskers

I didn't want everything I was developing to depend upon people coming to my farm.

That would have placed a geographical limitation on a vision that had never been geographically limited.

The people I had come to know through my online communities lived in many different places. They had their own homes, their own families, their own knowledge, and their own opportunities to serve.

What interested me was how we could make what one community learned available to another.

If someone developed a useful way of growing food, why shouldn't that knowledge benefit families elsewhere?

If a group of people learned how to organize emergency communications in their area, why shouldn't another community be able to learn from their experience?

If people studying Scripture together discovered something worth examining, why shouldn't they be able to share it with other people who were studying the same passages?

Technology could help make those things possible.

But the purpose wasn't to draw everyone into a single location or make everyone dependent upon a single teacher.

The purpose was to help people become better equipped to serve wherever they already lived.

That is why the farm and HomeGroups belonged together in my thinking.

One provided a place to explore and practice some of the things I believed were important.

The other provided a way to extend the opportunities for learning, fellowship, and collaboration beyond that place.

## What We Were Really Growing

There were many things I wanted to grow at Big Whiskers.

There were many systems I wanted to develop and many things I wanted to learn.

But I think the most important thing we can help grow in any community is the ability of its people to care for one another.

In John 21, Yeshua repeatedly directs Peter's attention to the responsibility of caring for His sheep.

I don't understand that responsibility to be fulfilled simply by standing in front of people and telling them things.

It requires taking an interest in their lives.

It requires recognizing their needs, encouraging them, sharing what we know, and helping them develop the ability to do those same things for others.

The farm gave me opportunities to think about that responsibility in a very practical environment.

And it gave me another way to understand the relationship between the things I had been pursuing throughout my life.

I had been developing technology.

I had been studying Scripture.

I had been learning about sustainable living.

I had been trying to provide for my children.

And I had been developing relationships with people who wanted to learn and work together.

Those endeavors didn't need to remain separate.

They could become part of a way of life.

What I wanted for HomeGroups was not simply a means of bringing more people to a place where I could teach them.

I wanted to help develop communities in which people could teach, learn from, and serve one another.

And then take what they had learned home.

Because the vision was never limited to my home.

Or to my farm.
