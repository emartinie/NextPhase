# Chapter Seven — Sustainable Living

When I became a father, the future took on an entirely different meaning.

It was no longer just my future.

I was responsible for providing a home for my children, protecting them, and doing everything within my ability to prepare them for the world they would eventually have to live in without me.

That responsibility extended beyond earning a living.

I could work hard, advance in my career, provide a comfortable home, and give my children opportunities that I never had growing up. But what if the world in which I was doing all those things was changing in ways that threatened the very future I was working to provide?

That was a significant part of what led me to Facebook in 2008.

I wasn't merely interested in what was happening in the world.

I had children who were going to inherit it.

## Trying to Change the Future

I had been researching things that concerned me for years before I ever joined Facebook.

And as I became increasingly aware of what I believed was happening in our country and throughout the world, I felt a responsibility to share what I was learning.

I became what I would describe as a digital warrior.

I spent considerable time gathering information, researching subjects, sharing documents and news articles, and trying to bring things to the attention of people who might otherwise never encounter them.

I wasn't doing all of that because I had given up on the future.

I was doing it because I hadn't.

I wanted to change the direction in which I believed things were going. I wanted people to become informed, recognize their responsibilities, and do something about the conditions that concerned us.

But there was another side to that responsibility.

While I was trying to help change the world my children would inherit, I also needed to consider how I could prepare my own home for circumstances that might be beyond my ability to change.

Those two endeavors were not contradictory.

I could work toward a better future while preparing my family for an uncertain one.

## More Than Prepping

At some point, the word *prepping* became increasingly popular.

People were talking about it on television, on the internet, in churches, and in conversations about prophecy and the future of our country.

But I wasn't particularly comfortable with the mindset that often accompanied those conversations.

There was a great deal of fear.

And when I looked at Scripture, I saw something different.

I saw wisdom.

I saw responsibility.

I saw provision.

And I saw love.

Years ago, on the Sustainable Living page of my HomeGroups website, I wrote:

> However, there is a concept in the Bible that is similar- One based in LOVE and wisdom, not fear.

I also wrote:

> Worldly provisions are of no value without Spiritual preparedness.

That distinction was important enough to me that I placed the spiritual discussion before the enormous collection of physical preparedness resources I had assembled.

I wanted people to understand why we should prepare before they began learning how to prepare.

There is a difference between storing food because I am afraid of what might happen to me and storing food because I have people I love who depend upon me.

There is also a difference between preparing to protect what I have from everyone around me and preparing to have something available when someone around me needs help.

What kind of people are we becoming as we prepare?

That question is every bit as important as what we are preparing for.

## The Wisdom of Scripture

The Bible doesn't present wisdom and dependence upon our Creator as opposing ideas.

Proverbs 6:6–8 directs us to consider the ant, which gathers its provisions in their season without someone standing over it and telling it what to do.

Proverbs 21:20 contrasts the provisions stored in the house of the wise with the behavior of the foolish person who consumes everything he has.

Proverbs 22:3 describes the prudent person who recognizes danger and takes refuge, while the inexperienced continue on and suffer the consequences.

And Proverbs 30:25 again directs our attention to the ant, a creature of little strength that nevertheless prepares its food in the summer.

These passages are not necessarily about the end of the world.

They are about how to live in the world.

Why should we wait until an emergency arises to begin developing the knowledge, habits, and resources that could help us through it?

Why should I wait until my children need something to begin thinking about how I might provide it?

But Scripture also gives us another responsibility.

Deuteronomy 22:4 says:

> Thou shalt not see thy brother's ass or his ox fall down by the way, and hide thyself from them: thou shalt surely help him to lift them up again.

Notice that this instruction isn't about taking care of my own possessions.

It is about my responsibility when I encounter someone else having difficulty with theirs.

I am not supposed to hide myself from that responsibility.

That is where my understanding of preparedness begins to extend beyond my own home.

## Start Where You Are

One of the things I encountered repeatedly in conversations about preparedness was that people didn't know where to begin.

What should I do?

What should I buy?

What should I learn?

Where do I start?

My answer on the original HomeGroups website was simple:

> Start where you are with what you have.

I encouraged people to begin by organizing the things they already possessed.

Know where your important documents are. Know where the things you might need in an emergency are located. Have some understanding of what is available in your own home before you begin accumulating more things.

And anybody who knows me well probably understands why I wrote about my love of bags.

But the bags weren't the point.

The point was developing habits.

I had prepared personal documents for my own family, and I made resources available to encourage other families to think about doing the same.

Every home has different circumstances. Every family has different needs. Not everyone has the same amount of money, the same amount of space, the same abilities, or the same opportunities.

But everyone can begin somewhere.

Preparedness doesn't have to begin with buying property, building a shelter, or purchasing an enormous amount of equipment.

It can begin in the home you already have.

It can begin with learning something you didn't know yesterday.

It can begin with a conversation around your own kitchen table.

## A Library for Other Homes

My research eventually resulted in an enormous collection of documents.

I accumulated white papers, PDFs, instructional materials, books, and other resources covering a wide range of subjects related to emergency preparedness and sustainable living.

I organized that material into eleven categories on the HomeGroups website:

- Food and water
- Healthy exercise
- Herbal remedies
- Home canning and food storage
- Homesteading
- Natural disaster and environmental survival
- Off-grid living
- Personal documents for families
- Survival retreats and shelters
- Survival skills
- Ultimate survival information

The downloadable archives were so large that I described them on the website as containing several terabytes of information.

I even suggested that people without sufficiently fast internet connections go to their public libraries before attempting to download them.

To my knowledge at the time, it was one of the largest collections of freely accessible preparedness information assembled in one place.

But the size of the collection wasn't what made it important.

I wanted other people to have access to information that had taken me years to accumulate.

Someone shouldn't necessarily have to spend years researching how to preserve food, provide clean water, prepare for a natural disaster, or organize important family documents simply because they didn't know where to begin.

And I wanted to make those resources available within an environment that was foundationally Scripture-based.

There were already countless places on the internet where people could learn about preparedness.

What I wanted to help people understand was how those things related to the lives we were called to live as believers.

## A Lifestyle Rather Than a Stockpile

There was another sentence I wrote on that original website that I think explains much of what I was trying to accomplish:

> This however is more about changing habits and lifestyles than storing up stuff. And it is hard to do that alone.

It is hard to do that alone.

Think about how much there is to learn.

One person may know how to grow food. Another may know how to preserve it. Someone else may understand electrical systems, communications, construction, mechanics, or healthcare.

One family may possess resources that another family doesn't have.

One person may have knowledge that could save another person years of trial and error.

Why should every household have to independently acquire all of that knowledge?

Why should every family have to face every difficulty alone?

This is one of the reasons I believed sustainable living belonged within HomeGroups.

Imagine a group of families studying Scripture together and also learning how to provide for their households.

Imagine those same families sharing what they know, helping one another develop practical skills, and becoming familiar enough with one another to recognize when someone needs assistance.

They don't have to wait for a disaster to begin doing those things.

They can begin doing them as part of the ordinary lives they live.

And in doing so, they are developing something that cannot simply be purchased and stored on a shelf.

Relationships.

## Communication

There is another aspect of preparedness that has always been particularly interesting to me because of my background in technology.

Communication.

Having information available is one thing. Being able to communicate with other people when you need them is another.

We have become accustomed to being able to pick up a phone and contact almost anyone we know.

But what happens when the infrastructure that makes that possible becomes unavailable?

How do I communicate with my children?

How do families communicate with one another?

How do members of a community know who needs assistance?

My interest in amateur radio and emergency communications belongs within that same understanding of HomeGroups.

The objective isn't merely to possess radios or learn how to operate them.

It is to develop the ability to communicate with people with whom we already have relationships.

As I write this book, we are developing capabilities within the HomeGroups application that reflect those very concerns.

We are exploring how to make study resources available without requiring an internet connection. We are working on ways for people to connect geographically, pray for one another, develop fellowships, and incorporate amateur radio resources into their communities.

These may sound like entirely different features to someone looking at a list of things an application can do.

To me, they belong together.

They represent different ways of helping homes and the people who live in them become better equipped to take care of themselves and one another.

## My Home and Your Home

I have already explained what having a home meant to me as a child and what providing a home meant to me as a father.

When I began researching preparedness, my own home was naturally at the center of my concerns.

I had children to feed.

I had children to protect.

I had children whose future mattered more to me than my own.

But if I believed what Scripture taught me about loving my neighbor, I couldn't allow my understanding of preparedness to end with my own family.

What about the father who didn't know where to find the information I had accumulated?

What about the mother who was concerned about the same things but didn't know where to begin?

What about the family that didn't have the resources available to mine?

What about the person who didn't have a family to turn to at all?

The knowledge I acquired for the benefit of my own home could benefit other homes.

And the relationships that developed between those homes could benefit mine.

That is not a picture of isolation.

It is a picture of community.

I didn't want to build a community of people whose primary objective was to hide from the world.

I wanted to help develop communities of people who were equipped to live in it, serve within it, and work toward making it better.

I wanted people to prepare spiritually and physically, not simply for the possibility of difficult circumstances, but for the responsibility of helping one another through them.

We can begin with what we have.

We can begin where we are.

We can begin in our own homes.

But we don't have to end there.

And we don't have to do it alone.
