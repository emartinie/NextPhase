# Chapter Three — Everything Changed When I Became a Father

I knew I was going to be a father long before I ever saw my daughter.

I knew Zoe was growing inside her mother's womb. I knew that someday there would be another human being in my life, someone I would be responsible for, someone who would depend on me for things I hadn't yet begun to understand.

I had months to think about it.

But knowing something is going to happen and experiencing it are two entirely different things.

The moment I saw Zoe, everything changed.

I don't know that I could have explained what happened to me at the time. I only know that something inside me changed so completely that the things I had considered important just moments earlier suddenly seemed different.

The world around me hadn't changed.

I had.

For the first time in my life, I was looking at someone whose life mattered more to me than my own.

It wasn't something I had to think about. I didn't have to weigh my responsibilities or make a conscious decision to put her first.

I saw my daughter.

And I was her daddy.

Until that moment, much of my understanding of fatherhood had been shaped by the fathers I had known, the fathers who had come and gone, and especially the father I hadn't had.

My biological father had been absent from my life. Other men had occupied that place at different times. Some had given me wonderful memories. Others had contributed to the instability that characterized so much of my childhood.

I had spent years knowing what it felt like to need a father.

Now, suddenly, I was one.

And the question that had followed me through so much of my life began to change.

It was no longer simply, *What kind of father should I have had?*

It was, *What kind of father am I supposed to be?*

That question would influence nearly everything I did afterward.

I didn't become a perfect man when Zoe was born. I didn't suddenly possess all the wisdom necessary to raise a child. I still had the same past, the same weaknesses, and many of the same questions.

But I had something I hadn't possessed before.

A responsibility that I wanted more than anything to fulfill.

And I knew exactly where I wanted to begin.

I wanted my children to have a home that never went away.

Those words might not sound extraordinary to someone who grew up in the same house, surrounded by familiar people, knowing that tomorrow would look much like yesterday.

But they meant everything to me.

I knew what it was like to move from place to place. I knew what it felt like when relationships changed and the place you called home changed with them. I knew what it meant to be kicked out, to run away, and to find myself without a home at all.

I didn't want that for my children.

I wanted them to have a place where their memories could accumulate instead of constantly being interrupted. A place where their belongings belonged, where their rooms were familiar, and where they could grow up without wondering when everything might change again.

I wanted them to take that stability for granted.

Not because I wanted them to be ungrateful, but because I believed children should be free to spend their childhood being children.

They shouldn't have to worry about the things adults are supposed to take care of.

And there was something else I wanted them to take for granted.

Daddy came home every single night.

I cannot overstate how important that was to me.

I wanted my children to know that when their father left for work, he was coming back. That when evening arrived, Daddy would be there. That they didn't have to wonder whether he had disappeared or whether something had happened that would take him away from them.

A home that never went away.

And a daddy who came home every single night.

Those were not simply things I hoped to give my children.

They were things I gave them.

For years, I believe I did an extraordinary job of protecting my children from many of the experiences that had made my own childhood so difficult.

That is something I am proud of.

I don't say it because I believe I was a perfect father. I wasn't. No father is.

I say it because there is a difference between recognizing what was missing from your childhood and actually doing something about it when you have children of your own.

I had lived long enough to know how easily patterns could repeat themselves.

I wanted to interrupt those patterns.

And I did.

As my family grew, so did my understanding of what it meant to be a father.

When my son came into the world, I wasn't encountering fatherhood for the first time. But I was discovering something else about it.

Each child is a person, not simply another opportunity to repeat what worked before.

Children have different personalities, different interests, different needs, and different ways of understanding the world.

Being their father meant learning who they were.

It meant paying attention.

It meant recognizing that my responsibility wasn't to manufacture two people who thought and behaved exactly as I did. It was to help two individuals grow into the people they were becoming.

That understanding would continue developing long after their childhoods.

And somewhere along the way, fatherhood began changing the way I thought about God.

Before Zoe was born, I had often understood God as Father through the experiences of a child who had needed a father.

I thought about what a father should be, what a father should provide, and what it meant to have someone who would never abandon you.

Those questions were deeply personal.

But after becoming a father myself, I began looking at the relationship from the other direction.

Instead of thinking only about what I needed from my heavenly Father, I began thinking about what my heavenly Father might want for His children.

What does a father desire for someone he loves?

What does it mean to protect a child while also preparing that child to live independently?

Why does a father establish boundaries?

Why does he give instructions?

Why does he sometimes allow a child to struggle with something he could easily resolve himself?

And what does it mean to love someone so deeply that their well-being becomes more important than your own comfort?

I didn't answer all those questions the day Zoe was born.

Some of them took years to develop.

Others are questions I am still asking.

But fatherhood gave me an entirely different perspective from which to approach them.

It also changed the way I understood commandments.

When I tell my children to do something, or warn them against something, I don't want obedience merely for the satisfaction of being obeyed.

I want what is good for them.

Sometimes they may understand my reasons. Sometimes they may disagree with me. Sometimes they may believe I am being unnecessarily restrictive.

And sometimes I may be wrong.

But the purpose of loving instruction is not to demonstrate power. It is to help someone flourish.

That realization influenced the way I began thinking about the Creator's instructions to His children.

I had spent years studying the Torah, examining commandments, and trying to understand the relationship between obedience, covenant, and faithfulness.

Fatherhood made those questions personal in a new way.

I began to understand that the relationship mattered as much as the instruction.

A child who obeys out of terror is not experiencing the same relationship as a child who trusts that the person giving the instruction loves him.

And a father who demands obedience without cultivating love and trust has misunderstood something essential about his responsibility.

That doesn't mean love eliminates boundaries or that good intentions make every decision correct.

It means the relationship is not incidental to the instruction.

It is the reason the instruction matters.

I also began to understand something about success.

Before becoming a father, I had ambitions. I wanted to accomplish things. I wanted to build something meaningful, to develop my abilities, and to make something of my life.

Those ambitions didn't disappear when I had children.

In many ways, they became stronger.

But their purpose changed.

Providing for my family became more important than simply accumulating accomplishments. Building a career mattered because of what that career could make possible for the people I loved.

I eventually reached professional heights that the young man I had once been could scarcely have imagined.

But a title couldn't make me a good father.

Neither could a paycheck.

A career could help provide a home, but it couldn't replace the person who was supposed to come home to it.

That distinction mattered to me.

I wanted my children to have the benefits of my work without losing their father to it.

And as much as I wanted to provide for them, I wanted them to know me.

I wanted them to be able to talk to me.

Not merely when they had done something good, or when they needed permission, or when there was a problem that required an adult to solve it.

I wanted them to know that their father was someone they could approach with questions, fears, mistakes, and things they might not feel comfortable discussing with anyone else.

I knew what silence could do to a family.

I had grown up around things that nobody wanted to talk about.

I didn't want my children to believe that difficult subjects were forbidden or that telling the truth would cost them their relationship with me.

I wanted them to know that they could tell Daddy the truth.

Even when it was uncomfortable.

Especially then.

Over time, that became one of the things I valued most about being their father.

Not simply having children who loved me, but building relationships in which they could trust me with the things that mattered to them.

I wasn't trying to raise children who would need my permission forever.

I was trying to raise people who would know they could always come to me.

And those are very different goals.

There is something else fatherhood taught me that I don't think I could have understood beforehand.

You cannot measure your love for your children solely by what you are willing to give them.

Sometimes love is measured by what you are willing to give up.

Time. Comfort. Convenience. Opportunities. Your own preferences.

And sometimes the sacrifice doesn't feel like a sacrifice at all.

Sometimes it simply feels like being Daddy.

I don't mean that fathers should cease to be individuals or abandon every personal ambition.

I mean that when you love your children, the question of what matters most changes.

Their happiness matters.

Their security matters.

Their future matters.

And the person you are becoming matters, because you are helping shape the world in which they are growing up.

I had spent much of my life trying to understand the events that shaped me.

Now I was participating in the events that would shape someone else.

That realization was both humbling and extraordinary.

It made me think differently about the past.

It made me think differently about the future.

And it made me think differently about what it meant to love.

My children are adults now.

They have their own lives, their own questions, their own relationships, and their own decisions to make.

I cannot raise them as though they were still little children, and I wouldn't want to.

But becoming adults did not make them any less my children.

Nor did it make me any less their father.

The responsibilities change. The conversations change. The ways I can help them change.

But something that began the moment I saw Zoe has never changed.

Their lives matter to me more than my own.

I still want to protect them.

I still want them to know they can come to me.

I still want to be someone they can trust, even when they disagree with me, even when they make mistakes, and even when I don't have the answers they need.

I cannot promise that I will always know what to say.

I cannot promise that I will always make the right decision.

But I can promise that being their father is not a role I intend to retire from.

When I think about the things I have accomplished in my life, there are many that once seemed important.

Some still do.

But none of them changed me the way that first glimpse of my daughter did.

Before that moment, I had spent much of my life wondering what kind of father I should have had.

After that moment, I had a much more important question to answer.

*What kind of father will my children have?*

And for the rest of my life, I intend to keep answering it.
